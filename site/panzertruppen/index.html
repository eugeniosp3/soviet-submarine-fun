Subs T-34 Panzers Climate Deep

A condensed reading of a Schiffer sourcebook

The Wedge

On 1 September 1939 the field army had 87 Panzer IIIs and 1,026 Panzer Is. The force that stunned the world was not a fleet of gun tanks. It was an idea riding mostly machine-gun hulls, then Czech loot, then thinner divisions.

Thomas L. Jentz Panzertruppen 1 · 1933–1942 About a 15-minute read

In November 1933 the German army’s entire tank park was four Grosstraktor — two of them dead — four Leichttraktor, and six Kleintraktor chassis. The first company at Zossen trained on agricultural tractors. Six years later the same army overran Poland in a month. The trick was not the tank. The trick was pretending the tractor was a tank until the tank existed — and then discovering, in Russia, that the tank still wasn’t enough.

Jentz spent twenty-five years in war diaries, K.St.N., and the Captured Records Section. He does not interpret. Guderian’s Panzer Leader, he says, left the small questions unanswered. How many of each type were actually with the troops on the morning? What did a company write down after the fight? Volume 1 is the offensive phase, through October 1942. The long guns people remember arrive in 1942, after the inventory that started Barbarossa has already been written off.

Chapter 01

The agricultural tractor

Versailles · Kama · 1926 — 1935

Versailles forbade Panzerwagen. That, Jentz writes, “simply magnified the importance of having tanks in any modern army.” April 1926: specifications for a tank disguised as Armeewagen 20. Six Grosstraktor assembled in secret at Rheinmetall, Unterlüß, August 1928 to July 1929, then shipped to Kama, near Kazan. Four Leichttraktor, 1930, same trip. Rear sprockets throw tracks; the next tank is specified with front drive. Krupp copies a Carden-Loyd. July 1933: 135 chassis, code name Landwirtschaftlicher Schlepper — painted Feldgrau, no turret, an agricultural tractor. Five extra firms get three hulls each so Germany will have an industry.

1 November 1933: Kraftfahrlehrkommando Zossen, one Lehrtrupp, men back from Kazan. 150 Schulfahrgestell — driver hulls — begin arriving in January 1934. August 1935, Munster: they assemble a whole experimental division around 318 machine-gun tanks, 15 radio tanks, and four museum Grosstraktor pretending to be a schwere Kompanie. 4,025 wheeled vehicles, 481 tracked. Distance driven: 275,000 km, about 600 km each. 27 mechanical breakdowns. Lutz signs the report: a Panzer-Division so assembled “appears to be operationally suitable.” Smallest useful front for a whole division: about three kilometres. Do not sprinkle battalions across the infantry. Keep them in a division. Give the commander a radio.

15 October 1935 they take the covers off three divisions — 1. Weimar, 2. Würzburg, 3. Berlin. Jentz’s footnote: this is not the birthday. It is the day secrecy could be dropped. Any earlier date would have admitted the units already existed.

Chapter 02

Three divisions, one argument

1935 — 1938

Then the army spends three years not building more divisions. Beck wants 48 Abteilungen and a 3.7 cm in two-thirds of the light tanks; tank-versus-tank will be the rule. The Allgemeine Heeresamt wants infantry-support machine-gun tanks at one-third the price — 150,000 RM — and says 36 Abteilungen is the realistic ceiling. The Waffenamt says a mature 3.7 cm design is not ready: about 100 Panzer III a month from 1 January 1938, about 3,000 by 1 October 1940, if the drawings freeze. Rushing produces scrap. Machine tools: about 20 million RM per factory, extra machines not in until 1 April 1937. Keep building I and II or the workforce evaporates.

Result: they keep building I and II. Platoon cut from seven tanks to five on Beck’s insistence. By 1 October 1937 they have issued 1,468 Panzer I, 238 Panzer II, and 12 Panzer III. A year later the Panzer I number has not moved. There is nowhere else to put them. Independent brigades, four motorized infantry divisions, three leichte Divisions with a single truck-borne Abteilung. Guderian, now Chef der Schnellen Truppen, will call it a setback. Brauchitsch, 24 November 1938, finally writes the real order: nine Panzer-Divisions, convert the leichte, tanks that can take a 3.7 cm hit.

One year before Poland: 59 Panzer IIIs in the whole army. The “Blitzkrieg tank” is still a prototype in inventory. Appendix B, first paragraph: “Tactical coordination through communication was a primary factor in the ability of the Panzertruppen to defeat superior armed and armored enemy tank forces.” 1940–42: over 55% of the tanks in a Panzer-Regiment had receivers only. Leaders talk. The rest listen. Kill the commander’s tank and the net dies.

Chapter 03

Borrowed tanks

Spain · Austria · Prague · 1936 — 1939

October 1936: first lift, 32 Panzer I and one kleine Befehlswagen. Thoma’s men are advisors, not combatants. By 30 October, Spanish crews are in action. The published figure is “about 150 Panzer I.” From production and inventory Jentz will only stand behind a maximum of 4 command hulls and 88 Panzer I — half Ausf.A, half Ausf.B. No Panzer II in the records. The T-26s sit at a thousand metres and shoot them up. S.m.K.H. penetrates at 120–150 m, not at 200. Fuller in The Times, 8 April 1937: the light tank is “a mobile coffin.” L’Intransigeant, 20 April: “The German Panzer division has failed before it was even put into service.” Mechanical: Panzer I Krupp engines 5,000–8,000 km. Reliable. Useless.

The useful loot is in Prague. Anschluss, March 1938: the Austrian schnelle Division becomes 4.leichte, Panzer-Abteilung 33. Occupation of Czechoslovakia, March 1939: Škoda L.T.M.35 issued to Panzer-Regiment 11 (122 “Skoda-Panzer” by 22 July) and Abteilung 65 (64 by 5 June). Germans add a loader — Czech crew was three — and rebuild each tank for FuG 5 and FuG 2. The 35(t) replaces the Panzer III in those units. L.T.M.38: a Czech order of 150, completed and delivered to the Heeres from May 1939. These become 7. and 8. Panzer’s main tank.

Chapter 04

Poland was a rehearsal with the wrong tanks

1 — 25 September 1939

Seven Panzer-Divisions, four leichte, two independent Abteilungen, five armies. White crosses on turret fronts. Panzer-Regiment 35 and 36 in 4. Panzer have zero IIIs; they fight the campaign on I, II, and a handful of IVs. Mlawa, day one: Panzer-Regiment 7 (Kempf) hits a rail-track barricade that was not on the aerial photos. 72 of 164 tanks gone. Kempf radios that an attack here is hopeless. Jentz: this was the exception.

Warsaw, 9 September: Panzer-Regiment 35 starts 120, 57 still operational that evening, 30 remaining lost. Eight dead, fifteen wounded. 400 km in eight days to the city; then the city. Polish 7.92 mm anti-tank rifle “cleanly penetrated” Panzer I and II fronts at 100 m. Panzer I is “outdated.” III and IV “good” but need heavier armour. The leichte Division’s truck-borne Abteilung is a traffic jam at every blown bridge.

Reassessed total write-offs: 89 I, 83 II, 7 35(t), 7 38(t), 26 III, 19 IV, 5 command tanks. Appendix A’s monthly reported losses are higher; Jentz wants the later write-off line. The leichte Divisions are already scheduled for conversion. Poland only delays it a few months. 1.leichte becomes 6. Panzer on 12 September and keeps the 35(t) regiment. 3.leichte → 8. Panzer, 16 October. 2.leichte → 7. Panzer, 18 October. 4.leichte → 9. Panzer, 3 January 1940. Ten Panzer-Divisions for France.

Chapter 05

Hatches open

10 May — 22 June 1940

Ten divisions. Seven of them stacked in Heeresgruppe A for the Meuse. The 3. and 4., still I/II divisions, are sent into Belgium to pick a fight with the 3e DLM — about 90 Somua, 140 Hotchkiss. Whole Heeres, 1 May 1940: 1,077 I, 1,092 II, 143 35(t), 238 38(t), 381 III, 290 IV. For France, 3. and 4. Panzer still fight mostly I/II; 4. Panzer starts with 40 IIIs total.

Fall Gelb as a picture, not a word Combined arms on a poplar road. The 3.7 cm is bouncing. The hatches are open. Jentz’s after-action from Hannut: German gunners “observe with open hatches, identify targets, and then first go to the gunsight.” French tanks “leaderless, aimless, badly commanded.”

Hannut, 12 May, Panzer-Regiment 35 versus 3e DLM. 3.7 cm “bounce off, even at close range (100 meters)” on rounded armour. 5. Kompanie kills 8 of 11 Hotchkiss, mostly with 3.7 cm; Malguth kills three with a 2 cm. 12–14 May: the regiment claims 53 French tanks. It is not because the German tank is better. Jentz’s after-action from 1. Panzer in Fall Rot: even Panzergranate 40 fails on R35 and B1 bis beyond 300 metres. “Almost all of the hits rebounded.” They kill three at close range when both sides close. They win because they arrive in a body, with radios, and the French do not.

Chapter 06

Seventeen divisions, the wrong war

April — December 1941

Balkans first. Six Panzer-Divisions. Hairpin roads. Every brake worn out. Marburg and Kybfels sunk with all of Panzer-Regiment 3’s tracked vehicles. 21 June, Panzer-Regiment 31: only 17 III and 6 IV. 2. and 5. Panzer are not opening-day divisions.

Barbarossa opens with 17 Panzer-Divisions in four Gruppen. Whole Heeres, 1 June 1941: 877 I, 1,074 II, 170 35(t), 754 38(t), 350 III with 3.7 cm, 1,090 III with 5 cm L/42, 517 IV. The 5 cm III does not exist in the inventory table until July 1940 (17 issued). By Barbarossa it is the actual medium tank. 7. Panzer, Panzer-Gruppe 3, goes to war on 167 Czech 38(t) — more tanks than any other unit. Newer divisions (12., 19., 20.) issued 40-plus Panzer I Ausf.A because II production could not keep up. Guderian’s Gruppe is the only one that looks like the 1940 idea.

22 June 1941 Seventeen thinner divisions, last good light. Then the 26-ton and 52-ton tanks. Langermann, 4. Panzer, 22 October, after Orel: crews know they can be killed at long range and “achieve only a very minimal effect… in spite of special ammunition fired at close range.” 8.8 cm Flak and 10 cm guns are “big as barn door, unarmored.”

6. Panzer, 31 October: Panzer II average 11,500 km, 35(t) 12,500, IV 11,000. The 35(t) “can no longer be rebuilt. All of the components are worn out.” 30 November: Panzer-Regiment 11 has zero operational 35(t) or IV. 15 September: OKH still has almost no replacements in theater. 2. and 5. Panzer finally arrive — 19 divisions for the Moscow push, 2 October. Moscow stalls 5/6 December. Rostov abandoned at the end of November.

Roughly half the I/II/III park, two-thirds of the IVs, and almost the entire Czech fleet. Appendix A: 35(t) losses not even reported June–December 1941.

Chapter 07

The long gun winter

January — October 1942

The plan was 30 Panzer-Divisions. The Eastern Front interrupts. They get a handful of new numbers — 21. from 5.leichte in Africa, 22., 23., 24. from 1. Kavallerie, 25., 26., 27. — and then rob the north to feed the south. Spring 1942: Heeresgruppe Süd gets a third Abteilung by stripping Nord and Mitte. Except a few regiments, the rest are left with one Abteilung.

New K.St.N. 1 November 1941: leichte Kompanie on Panzer III (17); mittlere authorized 14 Panzer IV but most units are not allowed a 3. Zug, so they fight with 10. The long 5 cm Kw.K.39 L/60 into leichte Kompanien as available after December 1941. The long 7.5 cm Kw.K.40 L/43 into mittlere from April 1942. Panzer-Regiment 201 (23. Panzer) goes east with 34 II, 112 III, 32 IV; somewhat less than half the IIIs are L/60; 12 IV L/43, of which 6 had arrived by 12 May. I. Abteilung, 12–27 May, claims 79 Russian tanks, 45 of them T-34s.

Doctrine is now a dance: one III frontal, hull-down or zigzag; two III to the flank and rear with Pzgr.40 at short range; IV in front with Nebelgranaten. If outnumbered, “build a fire front” — even without penetrations the Russians “almost always break off.” They also issue the short 7.5 cm III Ausf.N, 19 August 1942: a 1942 infantry gun in a medium turret, because the L/60 line cannot fill every hole. Until German tanks arrive, one Abteilung per regiment on French 35S / 38H.

What to tell your friend

If you remember seven things from Jentz, remember these.

One. In November 1933 Germany had fourteen tanks, and some of those did not run. The first mass tank is an agricultural tractor with a turret bolted on.

Two. On the day they invade Poland the field army has 87 Panzer IIIs and 1,026 Panzer Is. The Czech 35(t) and 38(t) are why several “Panzer” divisions have gun tanks at all.

Three. Spain did not prove the Panzer I. It proved the T-26. Jentz’s count is 88, not 150, and no Panzer II went.

Four. France is won with open hatches and radios, against tanks the 3.7 cm cannot kill from the front. They still lose about 35 percent of the gun-armed park.

Five. They double the number of Panzer-Divisions in 1940 by splitting the tanks. Barbarossa is seventeen thinner divisions, and 7. Panzer goes to war on 167 Czech 38(t).

Six. By 22 December 1941, sixteen divisions in the north and centre can still start 75 Panzer IVs. The write-off list by New Year is most of the Czech fleet and half the IIIs.

Seven. The long 5 cm and the long 7.5 cm — the tanks in the posters — are 1942. Jentz’s first volume ends when those guns finally arrive, and when the offensive phase is already over.
