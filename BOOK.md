# 45 Feet

A condensed voyage through Jan Breemer’s *Soviet Submarines: Design, Development and Tactics* (Jane’s Information Group, 1989).

About a 15-minute read. Figures are the book’s, as of 1989.

---

In February 1851 three men sat in a leaking iron whale on the floor of Kiel harbour and waited. The water was only forty-five feet deep. That was deep enough to kill them. They waited six and a half hours, until the pressure inside equalled the pressure outside, then opened the hatch and shot to the surface like champagne corks. It was the first submarine escape anyone had ever seen.

A few years later the same inventor, Wilhelm Bauer, sold a dolphin-shaped boat to the Tsar. The crew walked two treadmill wheels — nearly seven feet across — to turn a propeller. On a good day they could crawl two hundred metres in seventeen minutes. Diving a single metre took half an hour.

This is a book about what happened after that.

---

## 1. The iron coffin (1850–1905)

Bauer’s first boat was called *Brandtaucher* — Diving Incendiary. On a public dive in Kiel the hull folded and the boat sank. After the wait, the three men lived. The wreck sat on the harbour floor until 1887. It is still in a museum in Dresden.

Russia wanted something that could sting a superior fleet in the Black Sea. In May 1855 Bauer was commissioned to build *Diable Marin* — Sea Devil — at St Petersburg. A dolphin hull, half-inch iron, two human treadmills, an auxiliary sail. Designed to go to 150 feet. In practice she was good for about 50. One hundred and thirty-three trials without mishap, then seaweed fouled the propeller and the crew got out. She later sank for good off Ochta.

The first wholly Russian boat was built by a St Petersburg photographer, Ivan Aleksandrovsky, between 1863 and 1866. It collapsed at too great a depth in 1877. The same year S. K. Dzhevetsky launched a four-metre steel egg propelled by bicycle pedals, with leather gloves built into the conning tower so the lone occupant could stick mines onto an enemy hull by hand. The Ministry of Coastal Defence ordered fifty. Then, in 1886, coastal defence passed from the Navy to the Army, and the little boats finished as pontoons and buoys. Official interest slept until Japan.

---

## 2. Gloves, drop collars, and a box of surprises (1904–1921)

When the Russo-Japanese War started, Japan had no submarines. Russia had one boat worth the name: Bubnov’s 113-ton *Delfin*. On 20 June 1904 she was overloaded with trainees, a paddle steamer threw a wave over the open tower, hydrogen and petrol exploded, and she foundered. Six men lived.

What followed was a shopping spree: Bubnov boats shipped in pieces on the Trans-Siberian; Holland boats; Lake boats; Germania boats from Kiel. Simon Lake smuggled *Protector* out of Bridgeport on a Saturday and lifted her onto a steamer at sea. The Lake *Alligator* class, promised as ocean boats, “invariably stood on end” on trials. Russian officers ripped out four engine cylinders and a useless gun, added ballast pumps, and got them to dive in five minutes. They still arrived too late for Tsushima. The war did something more important than sink ships. It committed Russia, permanently, to the submarine.

Between 1905 and 1914 the submarine grew up: 150 tons became 800, diving times fell from twenty minutes to five. Russia kept pace, and kept its talent for the fabulous. *Krab*, designed by a railroad engineer, was the world’s first purpose-built minelayer — her crew called her the box of surprises. *Pochtoyy* ran a petrol engine underwater on bottled air and left a long trail of bubbles. In 1909 someone proposed a 4,500-ton cruiser-submarine with 36 torpedo tubes. None of the monsters were built.

In 1915 Baltic boats fired fifty torpedoes and hit nothing. The fifteen German freighters they did sink were mostly shot up with deck guns. Average large-boat patrol: five or six days a month. British E-class boats did the real damage. Then revolution, scuttling, internment. By 1921 the Imperial submarine fleet had, an American report said, “ceased to exist.”

## 3. The mystery fleet (1921–1941)

The Tenth Party Congress rebuilt a navy out of scrap and leftover Leopards renamed Farm Labourer, Commissar, Poor Peasant. Strategy: delay an enemy landing long enough for the Red Army to arrive.

Then the numbers went mad.

| Year-end | Soviet submarines | Of them, Pacific |
|----------|-------------------:|-----------------:|
| 1932     | 20                | 0                |
| 1934     | 82                | 39               |
| 1936     | 143               | 57               |
| 1938     | 168               | 69               |
| 1940     | 205               | 87               |

By 1934 the Pacific already held more submarines than Japan. They were not a high-seas fleet. The bulk were medium Shchukas and tiny Malodki — 160 tons, sixteen men, two torpedoes loaded from outside — built in the Urals, trialled in the Caspian, sent to Vladivostok by rail.

The good boat of the 1930s was the Stalinetz (S-class), bought from a German front company in The Hague: a forerunner of the Type VII. The K-class “cruiser” was 1,500 tons, 15,000-mile range, two 100 mm guns, and a folding-wing aircraft too frail to take off at sea. Stalin wanted battleships. The submarine fleet, as ever, mixed the brand-new with the obsolete.

## 4. A war they could not win (1941–1945)

On 21 June 1941 the Soviet Navy owned the largest submarine fleet on earth: 213–218 boats. In the Baltic, not one was at sea at dawn.

Geography first: a shallow, frozen-half-the-year sea. Then the barrier. In 1943 the Finns and Germans hung the Walross net across the Gulf of Finland — thirty miles, 180 feet deep — and seeded the water with mines. Several Soviet boats tried to force it. None got through until Finland quit in September 1944. Twenty-two months locked in.

When boats did break out they still hunted the Tsarist way: sitting in a drawn square, firing one torpedo, sometimes broaching because a Shchuka’s tubes were slow to flood. German merchants in the Baltic sailed without escorts.

In the Black Sea the fleet became a trucking company, running cargo into Sevastopol. In the Arctic, K-boats dueled escorts with deck guns. The Pacific force sat out the war until the last eight days against Japan. No Pacific boat fired a shot in anger.

| Fleet | In service | Lost | Ships sunk | Tons per boat lost |
|-------|----------:|-----:|-----------:|-------------------:|
| Germany | 1,162 | 785 | 2,808 | 18,700 |
| United States | 288 | 52 | 1,178 | 93,500 |
| USSR (inferred) | 272 | 109 | ~160 | 3,692 |

Two of every five Soviet boats that saw service were destroyed. The fleet did not change the war. Breemer: the Soviet Navy was not the Army’s assistant. It was the Army’s dependent.

## 5. The nightmare that wasn’t quite (1945–1960)

Western admirals were sure the next Battle of the Atlantic would be worse, because the Soviets had the Type XXI. Four complete boats went east at Potsdam. ONI once predicted 300 copies by 1950. They got four, used as laboratories.

Kuznetsov said 1,200 submarines. What they actually built was the Whiskey: 236 boats in six years, high freeboard, guns on the early marks, snorkel only from about 1955. NATO thought it was a Type XXI. It wasn’t. A Leninetz would have spent fifty days just commuting to the mid-Atlantic. The real plan was older than NATO: a moat against amphibious assault while the Army drove west.

Zulu: 26 boats, six of them the first ballistic-missile submarines. Quebec: 30 coastal boats, nicknamed Cigarette Lighter after engine fires. Whiskey Twin-Cylinder and Long Bin: missiles welded onto diesel hulls that then became unstable. One probably sank.

---

## 6. Atoms, rockets, a riddle (1960–1979)

*Leninsky Komsomol* commissioned 8 April 1958, four years after *Nautilus*. Then, unlike the Americans, the Soviets series-built three classes around the first reactor that worked: Hotel, Echo, November. Same plant, cousin to the icebreaker *Lenin*, which later had a melt-down.

The mission of HEN was to kill carriers. Then Victor, Yankee, Charlie. Charlie launched SS-N-7s from underwater: twenty-five nautical miles, about two minutes of warning. Echo II still had to sit on the surface for half an hour while a turbojet flew out to 150 miles under a Bear D’s eye.

Yankee was supposed to be Polarised. It was heavier, louder — a “rumbling train.” The first patrol within range of the American east coast came in 1968. The West assumed half the force would stay on station. The ceiling was five boats, even when 33 existed.

Why? McConnell read Gorshkov and heard withholding for the second day. MccGwire heard “bastions” — defended patches of the Barents and the Sea of Okhotsk. By 1980 almost everyone had converted to some version of the bastion. Soviet writing never uses the word.

---

## 7. The quiet revolution (1979–1989)

Brezhnev told Ford: if you build Trident, we will build Taifun. The West thought he meant a missile. He meant a submarine the length of a football field.

**Typhoon:** 170 metres, about 25,000 tons submerged, twenty solid-fuel missiles with eight warheads each, missiles forward of the sail, double hull, two reactors, 150 men. “We never dreamed that the thing would be that big,” said the U.S. Director of Naval Intelligence.

Beside it: Delta IV; Oscar (12,000 tons, 24 anti-ship missiles); Alfa (titanium, 40-plus knots, so deep most Western torpedoes could not follow); and Akula, the quiet one, sometimes nicknamed Walker after the spy ring.

Until Victor III, Soviet boats lagged American ones by ten to fifteen years in radiated noise. Akula and Sierra arrived in the neighbourhood of a Los Angeles. Toshiba sold milling machines. The Walkers sold SOSUS. A U.S. admiral told the Senate the physics was in the open literature.

Diesel never died. Kilo arrived as the Typhoons did — 2,500 tons, exportable. Five navies have built nuclear submarines. Only one refused to keep a diesel line.

By 1985 more than half the fleet was over twenty-one years old. Production had fallen from eighty-plus boats in 1955 to about six. Breemer’s last forecast: fewer boats, quieter, used not as a Maginot line but as a moving hunt — perhaps off Norfolk.

## What to tell your friend

1. The first Russian submarine story is three men at forty-five feet, waiting for the physics to let them out.
2. Russia kept more types, in service longer, than anyone else. Leopard beside Kasatka in 1915; Typhoon beside Whiskey in 1986.
3. They were often first: dedicated minelayer, sectional hull on a train, early periscope, drop collars, closed-cycle engine. First is not the same as good.
4. The world’s largest submarine fleet in 1941 was built for a coastal war and asked to do everything else. It did not starve Germany.
5. The West spent the 1950s fighting a Battle of the Atlantic Stalin was not equipped to start. The real trick was a moat, then missiles, then quiet.
6. By 1989 the boats had stopped sounding like trains. That, more than Typhoon’s tonnage, is the sentence that should have kept people up.
