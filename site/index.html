45 Feet

A field library. Submarines, tanks, mud, and the middle layer of war — condensed from the originals, in the grain of the plates.

Breemer · Tucker-Jones · Jentz · CMH · Glantz About 15 minutes each

The first book in this room is a Jane’s intelligence history of Soviet submarines. It begins, unforgettably, with three men sitting in a leaking iron whale on the floor of Kiel harbour. The water is only forty-five feet deep. That is deep enough to kill them. They wait six and a half hours, then shoot to the surface like champagne corks.

Four more books have been added to the shelf. Each is the same kind of thing: the whole argument of a serious volume, shortened, with the highest-voltage facts left standing, and new photographs made in the silver of the originals. No login. No audio. Sit with one of them the way you would sit with a plate in a Jane’s annual — slowly, and then tell someone.

Jan Breemer · Jane’s · 1989

45 Feet

Soviet submarines from a handmade iron coffin to a football-field under the ice. The world’s largest underwater fleet, and why it so often fought the wrong war.

Anthony Tucker-Jones · 2015

Thirty-Four

The tank that was not supposed to exist. Slope, diesel, two-foot tracks — and enough of them that the zoo did not matter.

Thomas L. Jentz · 1933–1942

The Wedge

How Germany built a tank force out of agricultural tractors, secret camps in Russia, and original combat reports — then spent it in the East.

German Report Series · 1952

−63°

Former panzer commanders, writing from memory: nature as the Red Army’s ally. Mud, mercury, ski troops, and a war Hitler did not pack for.

David M. Glantz · 1991

Deep Battle

The missing layer between tactics and strategy. Tukhachevsky’s idea, the purge that killed it, the war that proved it, and the nuclear hangover after.

How to read them

Each page is a single scroll, about fifteen minutes at a talking pace. The photographs are newly made in the grain of the books — silver, gray, a little scratched — not the plates themselves. Diagrams and tables are the books’ numbers, set in type so they cannot lie. Where an author contradicts himself, the page says so.
